Anatomy of a negotiable instrument

A check is not just a check. Under Article 3 it is a draft — an order to a bank to pay — and it only earns the special powers of a negotiable instrument if every part does its job. Tap the numbered parts of the check to see what each one is, then try to break it.

1 · The drawee — First River Bank

A check is an order, and an order needs someone to order around. The drawee is the bank told to pay. A draft drawn on a bank and payable on demand is exactly what §3-104(f) calls a check.

Now try to break it

§3-104(a) sets the recipe. Flip a switch to sabotage the check and watch which requirement falls — and what happens to the verdict.

  • ✓An unconditional promise or order — nothing the payment waits on.
  • ✓A fixed amount of money — $500.00, in figures and in words.
  • ✓Payable to bearer or to order — “pay to the order of Luis Ortega” lets it travel hand to hand.
  • ✓Payable on demand or at a definite time — a check is payable on demand.
  • ✓No extra undertakings — the paper orders payment of money, and nothing else.
  • ✓Not stamped out of Article 3 — no conspicuous “not negotiable” statement killing it under §3-104(d).
✔NEGOTIABLE — Article 3 governs this paper

Every requirement in §3-104(a) is met, so this check can be negotiated — transferred to a new holder who can enforce it in their own name.

The law — §3-104(a)

“‘Negotiable instrument’ means an unconditional promise or order to pay a fixed amount of money … if it: (1) is payable to bearer or to order at the time it is issued …; (2) is payable on demand or at a definite time; and (3) does not state any other undertaking or instruction … to do any act in addition to the payment of money.”

And §3-104(d): a promise or order (other than a check) that carries a conspicuous statement that it is not negotiable is not an instrument under Article 3 at all.

✉The check is in Rosa's hands — nothing is settled yet

An uncashed check discharges nothing. §3-311 only bites when the claimant obtains payment of the instrument. Rosa is holding Dan's offer: take $1,800 as the whole story, or hold out for $2,400. Flip the “Rosa cashes the check” switch to see what the law does with her choice.

The law — §3-311(a) & (b)

“If a person against whom a claim is asserted proves that (i) that person in good faith tendered an instrument to the claimant as full satisfaction of the claim, (ii) the amount of the claim was unliquidated or subject to a bona fide dispute, and (iii) the claimant obtained payment of the instrument … the claim is discharged if … the instrument or an accompanying written communication contained a conspicuous statement to the effect that the instrument was tendered as full satisfaction of the claim.”

Two escape hatches for the claimant live in §3-311(c): a business that told debtors in advance to send disputed-debt mail to a designated office isn't bound when the check lands elsewhere — and any claimant who tenders repayment of the check amount within 90 days after payment undoes the discharge. And note §3-311(d): if the claimant actually knew the check was tendered in full satisfaction, the claim is discharged even without the conspicuous statement.

★Cashing is the signature. On a genuinely disputed bill, depositing a conspicuously marked “paid in full” check settles the whole claim — crossing out the words doesn't save the balance.
UCC § 1-308EXAMPLE 3 · ARTICLE 1 — RESERVATION OF RIGHTS

Sign it — without giving your rights away

Keystone Auto Parts just delivered your shop's order, but the invoice in the box adds a $180 “handling fee” and new return terms you never agreed to. The driver needs your signature on the delivery acceptance before he unloads. You need the parts today. Sign below — with or without the reservation line — and see what §1-308 does with each choice.

KEYSTONE AUTO PARTS — DELIVERY ACCEPTANCE

Received: 40 brake rotors, 12 alternators, shop supplies as listed. Invoice total includes a $180 handling fee and is subject to Keystone's revised return terms (Rev. 11/2026), which the receiver accepts by signing below.

Without prejudice — UCC §1-308 — all rights reserved, under protest ✕
✕Your Name
Receiver's signature — performance / acceptance of delivery
✎The pen is in your hand

Try it both ways: sign once plain, start over, then sign with the reservation line. The signature is the same — the legal footprint isn't.

The law — §1-308(a)

“A party that with explicit reservation of rights performs or promises performance or assents to performance in a manner demanded or offered by the other party does not thereby prejudice the rights reserved. Such words as ‘without prejudice,’ ‘under protest,’ or the like are sufficient.”

The exception that matters — §1-308(b): “Subsection (a) does not apply to an accord and satisfaction.” That is why the reservation trick fails on the “paid in full” check in Example 2: cashing it is governed by §3-311, and no “without prejudice” scrawled on the check revives the balance.

★Performing under an explicit reservation lets you keep the deal moving and keep your dispute alive. No reservation, and your performance can be read as agreement.
UCC §§ 3-501 · 3-409EXAMPLE 4 · ARTICLE 3 — PRESENTMENT & ACCEPTANCE

The life of a draft

Maria's Export Co. sold Luis a shipment of coffee, payable in 30 days. Instead of a promise, Maria wrote an order: a draft commanding First River Bank to pay Luis. Walk the paper through its life — presentment is how Luis knocks on the bank's door, and acceptance is the moment the bank itself becomes bound to pay.

Step 1 of 5Maria writes the draft

Maria signs a written order: “30 days after sight, pay to the order of Luis Ortega $4,000,” addressed to First River Bank. Because it is an order rather than a promise, §3-104(e) calls it a draft. Right now the bank owes nothing on it — nobody has even asked it to pay.

The law — §3-501(a) & §3-409(a)

“‘Presentment’ means a demand made by or on behalf of a person entitled to enforce an instrument (i) to pay the instrument made to the drawee or a party obliged to pay the instrument … or (ii) to accept a draft made to the drawee.” — §3-501(a)
“‘Acceptance’ means the drawee's signed agreement to pay a draft as presented. It must be written on the draft and may consist of the drawee's signature alone.” — §3-409(a)

Until acceptance, the drawee is not liable on the instrument at all (§3-408). The signature on the paper is what turns an order into the bank's own obligation — which is why a check a bank accepts becomes a certified check (§3-409(d)).

★Presentment is the knock. Acceptance is the drawee signing its own name to the debt. No signature on the draft, no obligation for the bank.
UCC §§ 9-203 · 9-310 · 9-501 · 9-509EXAMPLE 5 · ARTICLE 9 — SECURED TRANSACTIONS

The tractor loan: attach it, then perfect it

Farmer June needs a $60,000 tractor. Heartland Bank will lend the money — if it can lock in a security interest in the tractor. Article 9 runs in two acts: first the interest must attach (§9-203), then it must be perfected by filing (§9-310) so it beats the rest of the world. Work the gates in any order.

The law — §§9-203, 9-310, 9-501, 9-509

“A security interest is enforceable against the debtor and third parties with respect to the collateral only if: (1) value has been given; (2) the debtor has rights in the collateral or the power to transfer rights in the collateral to a secured party; and (3) … the debtor has authenticated a security agreement that provides a description of the collateral.” — §9-203(b)
“Except as otherwise provided … a financing statement must be filed to perfect all security interests and agricultural liens.” — §9-310(a)

For most collateral the financing statement goes to the state's central filing office — the Secretary of State (§9-501(a)(2)); land-related collateral like fixtures goes to the local real-property records instead. And nobody may file without the debtor's authorization — which the signed security agreement itself supplies (§9-509(a)–(b)). An unauthorized filing isn't just ineffective: §9-625(e)(3) backs it with statutory damages.

UCC § 1-103EXAMPLE 6 · ARTICLE 1 — THE CODE + THE COMMON LAW

Two rivers, one law

The UCC was never meant to be the whole river. §1-103(b) says the older principles of law and equity supplement the Code — they flow alongside it and fill its gaps — unless a specific provision of the Code displaces them. Toggle the dam and watch which stream decides the case.

Both streams reach the case: the Code rules where it speaks, and the principles fill the gaps it leaves.
🌊Supplement — not supplant

Say a buyer signed a deal because the seller lied about the goods. Article 2 governs the sale, but nothing in it has to reinvent fraud — §1-103(b) lets the common-law fraud principle flow in and do its work beside the Code.

The law — §1-103

“(a) The Uniform Commercial Code must be liberally construed and applied to promote its underlying purposes and policies, which are: (1) to simplify, clarify, and modernize the law governing commercial transactions; (2) to permit the continued expansion of commercial practices through custom, usage, and agreement of the parties; and (3) to make uniform the law among the various jurisdictions. (b) Unless displaced by the particular provisions of the Uniform Commercial Code, the principles of law and equity, including the law merchant and the law relative to capacity to contract, principal and agent, estoppel, fraud, misrepresentation, duress, coercion, mistake, bankruptcy, and other validating or invalidating cause supplement its provisions.”

UCC §§ 3-415 · 3-419 · 3-416 · 3-402 · 3-206 · 2-316EXAMPLE 7 · THE WORDS BESIDE YOUR NAME

Signature words: small print, big teeth

People rarely sign with just a name. They add words — without recourse, guarantor, under duress, no warranty, express or implied — hoping the words change what the signature does. Sometimes they do, exactly. Sometimes the Code ignores them completely. Luis is about to indorse the back of a note: tap a phrase to write it beside his name and read what the law does with each one.

BACK OF INSTRUMENT — INDORSE HERE
Luis Ortega
✕ Sign your name exactly as it appears on the face of the instrument

Just the name — the full indorser's promise

Signing alone makes Luis an indorser, and §3-415(a) attaches the standard obligation: if the instrument is dishonored, the indorser must pay the amount due to the person entitled to enforce it. No words added, no liability subtracted. Every phrase below is measured against this baseline.

✎Baseline: fully liable as indorser

A bare signature is the whole standard package — §3-415(a) obligation included.

The law — the sections behind the phrases

“If an indorsement states that it is made ‘without recourse’ or otherwise disclaims liability of the indorser, the indorser is not liable under subsection (a) to pay the instrument.” — §3-415(b)
“If the signature of a party to an instrument is accompanied by words indicating that the party guarantees payment … the signer is obliged to pay the amount due on the instrument to a person entitled to enforce the instrument in the same circumstances as the accommodated party would be obliged, without prior resort to the accommodated party…” — §3-419(e)
“The warranties stated in subsection (a) cannot be disclaimed with respect to checks.” — §3-416(c)

And on signatures for someone else, §3-402(b)(1): if the form of the signature “shows unambiguously that the signature is made on behalf of the represented person who is identified in the instrument, the representative is not liable on the instrument.” Leave that clarity off, and §3-402(b)(2) can make the signer personally liable to a holder in due course.
