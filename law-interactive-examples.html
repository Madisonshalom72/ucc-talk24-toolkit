A check is an order, and an order needs someone to order around. The drawee is the bank told to pay. A draft drawn on a bank and payable on demand is exactly what §3-104(f) calls a check.

§3-104(a) sets the recipe. Flip a switch to sabotage the check and watch which requirement falls — and what happens to the verdict.

  • ✓An unconditional promise or order — nothing the payment waits on.
  • ✓A fixed amount of money — $500.00, in figures and in words.
  • ✓Payable to bearer or to order — “pay to the order of Luis Ortega” lets it travel hand to hand.
  • ✓Payable on demand or at a definite time — a check is payable on demand.

✔NEGOTIABLE — Article 3 governs this paper

Every requirement in §3-104(a) is met, so this check can be negotiated — transferred to a new holder who can enforce it in their own name.

The law — §3-104(a)

“‘Negotiable instrument’ means an unconditional promise or order to pay a fixed amount of money … if it: (1) is payable to bearer or to order at the time it is issued …; (2) is payable on demand or at a definite time; and (3) does not state any other undertaking or instruction … to do any act in addition to the payment of money.”

And §3-104(d): a promise or order (other than a check) that carries a conspicuous statement that it is not negotiable is not an instrument under Article 3 at all.

An uncashed check discharges nothing. §3-311 only bites when the claimant obtains payment of the instrument. Rosa is holding Dan's offer: take $1,800 as the whole story, or hold out for $2,400. Flip the “Rosa cashes the check” switch to see what the law does with her choice.

The law — §3-311(a) & (b)

“If a person against whom a claim is asserted proves that (i) that person in good faith tendered an instrument to the claimant as full satisfaction of the claim, (ii) the amount of the claim was unliquidated or subject to a bona fide dispute, and (iii) the claimant obtained payment of the instrument … the claim is discharged if … the instrument or an accompanying written communication contained a conspicuous statement to the effect that the instrument was tendered as full satisfaction of the claim.”

Two escape hatches for the claimant live in §3-311(c): a business that told debtors in advance to send disputed-debt mail to a designated office isn't bound when the check lands elsewhere — and any claimant who tenders repayment of the check amount within 90 days after payment undoes the discharge. And note §3-311(d): if the claimant actually knew the check was tendered in full satisfaction, the claim is discharged even without the conspicuous statement.

The law — §1-308(a)

“A party that with explicit reservation of rights performs or promises performance or assents to performance in a manner demanded or offered by the other party does not thereby prejudice the rights reserved. Such words as ‘without prejudice,’ ‘under protest,’ or the like are sufficient.”

The law — §§9-203, 9-310, 9-501, 9-509

“A security interest is enforceable against the debtor and third parties with respect to the collateral only if: (1) value has been given; (2) the debtor has rights in the collateral or the power to transfer rights in the collateral to a secured party; and (3) … the debtor has authenticated a security agreement that provides a description of the collateral.” — §9-203(b)

Say a buyer signed a deal because the seller lied about the goods. Article 2 governs the sale, but nothing in it has to reinvent fraud — §1-103(b) lets the common-law fraud principle flow in and do its work beside the Code.

The law — §1-103

“(a) The Uniform Commercial Code must be liberally construed and applied to promote its underlying purposes and policies, which are: (1) to simplify, clarify, and modernize the law governing commercial transactions; (2) to permit the continued expansion of commercial practices through custom, usage, and agreement of the parties; and (3) to make uniform the law among the various jurisdictions. (b) Unless displaced by the particular provisions of the Uniform Commercial Code, the principles of law and equity, including the law merchant and the law relative to capacity to contract, principal and agent, estoppel, fraud, misrepresentation, duress, coercion, mistake, bankruptcy, and other validating or invalidating cause supplement its provisions.”

Signature words: small print, big teeth

People rarely sign with just a name. They add words — without recourse, guarantor, under duress, no warranty, express or implied — hoping the words change what the signature does. Sometimes they do, exactly. Sometimes the Code ignores them completely. Just the name — the full indorser's promise

Signing alone makes Luis an indorser, and §3-415(a) attaches the standard obligation: if the instrument is dishonored, the indorser must pay the amount due to the person entitled to enforce it. No words added, no liability subtracted. Every phrase below is measured against this baseline.

✎Baseline: fully liable as indorser

A bare signature is the whole standard package — §3-415(a) obligation included.

The law — the sections behind the phrases

“If an indorsement states that it is made ‘without recourse’ or otherwise disclaims liability of the indorser, the indorser is not liable under subsection (a) to pay the instrument.” — §3-415(b)
“If the signature of a party to an instrument is accompanied by words indicating that the party guarantees payment … the signer is obliged to pay the amount due on the instrument to a person entitled to enforce the instrument in the same circumstances as the accommodated party would be obliged, without prior resort to the accommodated party…” — §3-419(e)
“The warranties stated in subsection (a) cannot be disclaimed with respect to checks.” — §3-416(c)

And on signatures for someone else, §3-402(b)(1): if the form of the signature “shows unambiguously that the signature is made on behalf of the represented person who is identified in the instrument, the representative is not liable on the instrument.” Leave that clarity off, and §3-402(b)(2) can make the signer personally liable to a holder in due course.

UCC § 3-302EXAMPLE 8 · ARTICLE 3 — HOLDER IN DUE COURSE

The note that changed hands three times

Owen signed a $3,000 note to Priya for her “Six-Figure Secrets” business course. The course was a fake — recordings of someone reading blog posts out loud — so Owen has a real fraud defense and refuses to pay. But Priya didn't hold the note. She passed it on, and it kept moving. Tap each holder and run them through the §3-302 checklist: who took it the right way, and who gets to collect from Owen despite the fraud?

PROMISSORY NOTE
Maker: Owen Hart · Payee: Priya Nair
No. 0302
Pay to the
order of Priya Nair $ 3,000.00
Three thousand and 00/100 — — — — — — Dollars
Memo Six-Figure Secrets course

Holder 1 · Priya Nair — the payee herself

Priya took the note directly from Owen as payment for the course. A payee can be a holder in due course — but Priya is the one who ran the fake course. She has notice of Owen's defense because the defense is her own conduct. Against Priya, Owen's fraud defense works in full: she collects nothing.

  • ✓Took for value — she gave the course (such as it was) for the note.
  • ✗Without notice of a defense — she created the fraud herself; nobody knows about it better.
  • ✓Paper regular on its face — no forgery or alteration showing.
✘Priya is a mere holder — Owen's defense stops her cold

A holder who fails any line of the §3-302 checklist takes the instrument subject to the maker's personal defenses (§3-305(b), §3-306). Fraud in the inducement is exactly such a defense — against Priya, Owen pays nothing.

The law — §3-302(a)

“‘Holder in due course’ means the holder of an instrument if: (1) the instrument when issued or negotiated to the holder does not bear such apparent evidence of forgery or alteration or is not otherwise so irregular or incomplete as to call into question its authenticity; and (2) the holder took the instrument (i) for value, (ii) in good faith, (iii) without notice that the instrument is overdue or has been dishonored …, (iv) without notice that the instrument contains an unauthorized signature or has been altered, (v) without notice of any claim to the instrument …, and (vi) without notice that any party has a defense or claim in recoupment…”

The prize for qualifying is in §3-305(b): a holder in due course takes free of personal defenses — like fraud in the inducement — that would have defeated the original payee. Only the short list of “real” defenses in §3-305(a) (infancy, duress that nullifies the obligation, fraud in the factum, discharge in insolvency) survives against an HDC. And under §3-302(f), notice that arrives after the holder took the instrument doesn't count — timing is everything.

The law — §3-407(b) & (c)

“…a fraudulent alteration discharges a party whose obligation is affected by the alteration unless that party assents or is precluded from asserting the alteration.” — §3-407(b)
“A payor bank or drawee paying a fraudulently altered instrument or a person taking it for value, in good faith and without notice of the alteration, may enforce rights with respect to the instrument (i) in the case of an alteration of the amount, according to its original terms…” — §3-407(c)

“Original terms” is the whole game: the bank that paid in good faith can charge Marta's account for the check she actually wrote — $100 — and no more. The extra money is the bank's problem to recover from the alterer (who also faces §3-420 conversion liability and criminal forgery law). The exception is §3-406(a): a person “whose failure to exercise ordinary care substantially contributes to an alteration” is precluded from asserting it against someone who paid in good faith — draw a check with room to raise it, and the loss can shift onto you.

§4-403(a) makes timing the whole ballgame: the order must be “received at a time and in a manner that affords the bank a reasonable opportunity to act on it” before the bank pays. Run the race both ways — beat the check, and lose to it.

The law — §4-403(a), (b) & (c)

“A customer … may stop payment of any item drawn on the customer's account … by an order to the bank describing the item or account with reasonable certainty received at a time and in a manner that affords the bank a reasonable opportunity to act on it before any action by the bank with respect to the item…” — §4-403(a)

Two riders every drawer should know: the burden of proving loss from a payment made over a valid stop order sits on the customer (§4-403(c)) — and stopping payment stops the check, not the debt. If Dana genuinely owes the money, the payee can still sue on the underlying obligation; the stop order just forces the fight into the open instead of letting the money walk away first.

The law — §9-324(b), and the 20 days that belong to equipment

“…a perfected purchase-money security interest in inventory has priority over a conflicting security interest in the same inventory … if: (1) the purchase-money security interest is perfected when the debtor receives possession of the inventory; (2) the purchase-money secured party sends an authenticated notification to the holder of the conflicting security interest; (3) the holder of the conflicting security interest receives the notification within five years before the debtor receives possession of the inventory; and (4) the notification states that the person sending the notification has or expects to acquire a purchase-money security interest in inventory of the debtor and describes the inventory.” — §9-324(b)

Notice what's missing: no 20-day grace period. The famous 20 days live in §9-324(a), and they cover goods other than inventory — equipment like June's tractor in Example 5, where the PMSI lender may file up to 20 days after the debtor takes possession and still jump the line. Inventory is stricter — perfected at possession, plus advance notice — because inventory lenders and floor-planners rely on the filing record every single day, and the Code makes the PMSI seller warn them before the goods ever land.

L.A.W. COURSEEXAMPLE 12 · THE THREE GREAT JURISDICTIONS

Which court? Law, equity, or admiralty

From the L.A.W. course modules: Anglo-American justice grew three great streams. Common law courts award money damages under strict rules, historically with juries. Equity courts — born when petitioners begged the king's chancellor for fairness the law courts couldn't give — order people to do or stop doing things: injunctions, specific performance, foreclosure. Admiralty governs the sea: ships, cargo, salvage, and sailors. Six cases are waiting on the docket — send each one to its court.

CASE 1 OF 6 · SCORE 0

🏛The clerk is waiting

Read the case, ask what the person actually wants — money, an order to act or stop, or a ruling about the sea — and tap that court.

⚖

The framework — why three courts at all

Law grew from the king's courts and their writs: if your complaint fit a form and you wanted damages, the common law heard you — with a jury, and with little mercy for cases that didn't fit the forms. Equity grew beside it precisely because of that rigidity: subjects petitioned the Chancellor, the “keeper of the king's conscience,” and his court ordered what fairness required — stop the harm (injunction), honor the deal itself (specific performance), unwind the fraud — sitting without a jury and aiming at conscience rather than forms. Admiralty came from the law merchant of the sea: maritime contracts, collisions, salvage awards, cargo, and the special protections owed to seamen, administered under rules shared by seafaring nations.

Modern American courts have mostly merged law and equity into one courthouse — but the distinction still decides real questions every day: whether you get a jury, whether a judge can order action instead of money, and whether a maritime claim follows admiralty rules. Sort by the remedy and the subject, and the right door usually names itself.

★Ask two questions of any case: what remedy is wanted — money, or an order? — and where did it happen — land, or sea? Those two answers route nearly every case to its court.

The law — assignment, and the state rules that now surround it

The engine of the whole play is one sentence of contract law: contract rights are assignable unless the contract forbids it or the assignment would materially change the other party's burden. (The UCC states the same rule for goods contracts at §2-210; land contracts follow the common-law rule.) That is why the assignment clause is the whole game: with an anti-assignment clause — or no assignable contract at all — there is nothing to sell. What you sell is never the house. It is your equitable interest in the contract: the right to buy at the contract price.

Pennsylvania rewrote this play in 2024. Act 52 of 2024 (effective January 2025) amended the Real Estate Licensing and Registration Act to define a “wholesale transaction” as:

“…undertaking to promote the sale, exchange or purchase of an equitable interest or other interest in residential property with the intent to assign, sell or otherwise transfer the interest for a fee, commission or other valuable monetary consideration without having taken title as the owner of record.” — 63 P.S. § 455.201

Act 52 folds wholesale transactions into the Act's broker and salesperson definitions — wholesaling 1-to-4-unit homes is licensed activity in Pennsylvania — and every wholesale contract must carry four written disclosures (63 P.S. § 455.610): that the deal is a wholesale transaction, that the seller may get an independent appraisal and consult an unaffiliated licensee or a lawyer, that the seller has a 30-day right to cancel (until midnight of the 30th day after signing, or until the property is conveyed, whichever comes first), and that all money paid is refunded within ten business days of a cancellation. The cancellation right cannot be waived — and if the disclosures are missing, the seller can cancel at any time before conveyance. Philadelphia adds its own city Residential Property Wholesaler License on top (in force since January 2021).

Other states draw their own lines: in Illinois, more than one wholesale deal in a 12-month period requires a broker's license (225 ILCS 454); Oklahoma has required a license since 2021 to publicly market an equitable interest in a contract. And everywhere, the same trap waits: marketing a property you don't own as though you were its seller or the seller's agent is the conduct licensing laws were written against — the lawful pitch is the contract rights, disclosed as exactly that. State rules checked November 2026; this area of law is moving fast, so verify your state's current rule before you sign.

The subject-to play: the deed moves, the loan stays

A seller is behind on a mortgage she can no longer carry — a 3% loan from cheaper days. You offer the classic subject-to deal: the deed transfers to you, but her loan stays in her name, and you step in and make her payments. Nobody asks the bank. Three moments in this deal decide whether it was a rescue or a wreck — rule on each one.

MOMENT 1 OF 3 · SCORE 0

🏠The deed is on the table

In a subject-to deal the seller keeps the debt and you keep the payments. Every danger in the structure flows from that split — choose like you understand it.

The law — the due-on-sale clause, 12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3

“[A] ‘due-on-sale clause’ means a contract provision which authorizes a lender, at its option, to declare due and payable sums secured by the lender's security instrument if all or any part of the property, or an interest therein, securing the real property loan is sold or transferred without the lender's prior written consent…” — 12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(a)(1)

And §1701j-3(b)(1) gives that clause federal teeth: a lender may enter into or enforce a due-on-sale clause notwithstanding any state law to the contrary. Read that plainly: when the deed moves to an investor, the lender can call the entire loan due. Whether a given lender discovers a transfer, and whether it chooses to act, is the lender's business decision — it is not a legal protection you hold. Anyone who tells you “the Garn-St Germain Act stops them” has it backwards: the Act is the reason the clause works.

The Act does protect a short, closed list of transfers on homes with fewer than five units (§1701j-3(d)) — transfers on a borrower's death, to a spouse or children, in a divorce settlement, or into a living trust where the borrower stays a beneficiary. An investor's subject-to purchase is not on the list.

The working parts that protect real people in a real deal: the seller signs a written disclosure stating that the loan stays in her name, that late payments land on her credit, and that the lender may call the loan; a performance deed of trust (or equivalent security) lets her take the property back if you default; payments run through a third-party servicer so every payment is documented to both sides; and insurance and tax/insurance escrows are re-papered so coverage doesn't silently lapse when the deed moves. A mirror wrap — where the seller wraps her loan inside a new note from you on the same terms — is a genuine alternative structure some investors use, but it does not switch off the due-on-sale clause either. The paper that protects both sides is disclosure, not disguise.

The law — Howey, and Regulation D Rule 506

Why is any of this the SEC's business? Because a passive investor's interest in a syndicate is a security. The Supreme Court's test, from SEC v. W.J. Howey Co., 328 U.S. 293 (1946):

“…a contract, transaction or scheme whereby a person invests his money in a common enterprise and is led to expect profits solely from the efforts of the promoter or a third party…” — 328 U.S. at 298–299

Money in, common enterprise, profits expected from someone else's work — a syndicate share fits all three, whatever the paperwork calls it. Securities must be registered with the SEC unless an exemption applies, and the exemption nearly every real-estate syndicate uses is Regulation D, Rule 506, which comes in two flavors:

Rule 506(b) — the quiet door. Raise an unlimited amount from an unlimited number of accredited investors (plus up to 35 non-accredited investors who are sophisticated, with extra disclosure duties) — but no general solicitation or advertising, ever. The offering stays private because the marketing stays private.

Rule 506(c) — the loud door. General solicitation is allowed — ads, posts, public pages — but then every purchaser must be an accredited investor, and the issuer must take reasonable steps to verify that status: tax forms, brokerage statements, or a written confirmation from a CPA, attorney, or registered adviser. A checkbox where the investor simply claims “I'm accredited” is not verification.

Either way, the rights live in the paper: the PPM (private placement memorandum) discloses the deal and its risks, the operating agreement fixes the waterfall you just built, and the subscription documents are how each investor actually buys in. A Form D notice goes to the SEC within 15 days of the first sale. And the sponsor who takes the money takes on real duties with it — full disclosure, no misleading statements (the antifraud rules apply to every offering, exempt or not), and an obligation to run the deal as the documents promised. There is no version of this play where you “raise money from anybody with a post.” The post chooses the rule; the rule chooses who may buy.

Case 3 · The bank signs

A holder presents a time draft to the drawee bank for acceptance. The bank writes its signature across the face of the draft and hands it back. What is the bank now?

Case 4 · The race to the filing office

Heartland Bank lent June $60,000 against her tractor and took a signed security agreement — but never filed a UCC-1. A month later Valley Credit lent against the same tractor and filed immediately. June defaults. Whose claim on the tractor stands first?

Case 5 · The “without recourse” indorsement

Luis indorses a note “without recourse” and sells it to Ana. The maker defaults and the note is dishonored. Can Ana collect the amount of the note from Luis as indorser?

Case 6 · The raised check

Marta writes a check for $100. The payee raises it to $1,100, and the bank pays the full $1,100 in good faith, without noticing the alteration. How much of that may the bank charge to Marta's account?

Case 7 · The 30-day door

In Pennsylvania, a wholesaler signs a seller to a wholesale contract — but leaves the Act 52 disclosures out of the agreement entirely. The seller signs anyway. Two weeks later, she wants out. Can she cancel?

Case 8 · The called loan

An investor takes a deed subject to the seller's old 3% mortgage, back when rates are now 8%. The lender discovers the transfer and accelerates the loan under its due-on-sale clause. The investor protests that federal law bans due-on-sale enforcement. Who has the law right?

Case 9 · The public post
